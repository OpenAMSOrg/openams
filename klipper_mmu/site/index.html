klipper-mmu docs

Start here

Your MMU, run by its own microcontroller.

klipper-mmu is an optional add-on for Klipper. It moves the motion, sensing and safety of a multi-material unit onto the microcontroller wired to it. The host decides what to do (“load lane 2”). The unit decides when, every millisecond, from its own sensors.

  • 1 mscontrol tick on the unit
  • 2 sof host silence before the unit stops everything
  • 22units and variants supported in software
  • 12STM32 chips in the release builds

Read this first

Nothing here has been qualified on real MMU hardware yet. Every unit is a software candidate: it builds, boots, and passes the simulators. The default firmware will not move a motor. The motion-enabled firmware is an explicit opt-in. Check your pins, polarities, currents and emergency stop on your own bench before you trust it with a print.

What it is

In stock Klipper, the host plans every move ahead of time and the microcontroller plays the plan back. That is the right design for a toolhead. It is a poor fit for a filament feeder, which has to react to what its sensors see: a switch that trips, a buffer that fills, an encoder that stops clicking.

klipper-mmu adds a small control core to Klipper's own firmware. It uses Klipper's scheduler, drivers and communication. The core starts the motor drivers, generates steps through Klipper's stepper code, ramps speed up and down, follows the buffer, watches every switch, and stops everything when something is wrong.

  • Not a Klipper fork. You switch it on in make menuconfig. Switched off, Klipper builds exactly as before; this is checked on every merge.
  • One small driver patch. Klipper's drivers lacked a few functions the core needs, so klipper-mmu adds them as one explicit patch, pinned to one Klipper version. With klipper-mmu off, it changes nothing.
  • Works with OpenAMS. On the host, a klipper-mmu module connects the unit to OpenAMS, which picks lanes and spools and pauses the printer when needed.

Why the microcontroller

One number explains it. During a load, the filament tip reaches the extruder and the buffer starts to compress. The unit calls the filament “arrived” at 70% compression. On the OpenAMS buffer (15 mm of travel) that leaves 4.5 mm before it is fully compressed and the feeder starts grinding filament.

4.5 mm ÷ 200 mm/s full speed  = 22.5 ms to notice and stop
4.5 mm ÷  20 mm/s crawl      = 225 ms

The host would have to receive the reading over USB, notice it, and send a stop back, usually within 22 ms. The microcontroller reads the sensor every millisecond and stops the motor itself. So the unit loads fast, slows to a crawl before the toolhead, and stops on the reading, without waiting on anyone.

The same local loop makes other feedback useful. An encoder that stops clicking while the motor turns means slip or a jam, and the unit can react during the move instead of reporting it afterwards.

Who does what

The host (Klipper, OpenAMS and the klipper-mmu host module on your Raspberry Pi):

  • chooses the lane, the spool and the tool;
  • keeps your calibration and restores it at every restart;
  • turns firmware events into messages you can act on.

The unit (klipper-mmu on the MMU's board):

  • owns the feeder and selector drivers and every step they take;
  • speeds up, slows down, follows the buffer and detects arrival, every 1 ms;
  • stops on any fault, and stops if the host is silent for 2 s.

The host can ask for an operation. It cannot move a motor directly.

Where things stand

Done

22 units and variants on one shared control core: independent feeders, and linear, rotary, servo and indexed selectors. Supported units lists them.

Done

Release builds for 12 STM32 chips, and whole-system simulation of 21 units: the real firmware, Klipper and OpenAMS loading, following and unloading against a simulated MMU.

Real hardware: torque at speed, braking without lost steps, and qualification unit by unit.
